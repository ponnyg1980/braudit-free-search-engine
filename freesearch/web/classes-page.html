Why this matters more than the name

Most people choose a name carefully and then pick classes in the last two minutes before paying.

It is the wrong way round. The name is what you are protecting. The classes and terms are the protection itself. A registration is not a fence around your brand, it is a fence around your brand in the categories you named, and nowhere else.

There are 45 classes in total: 34 for goods, 11 for services. Within each class you choose specific terms, and those terms are what you would actually rely on in a dispute. Classes are broad. Terms are the detail. Both carry weight.

And the mistake that costs the most: you cannot add to a filed application.

You can remove classes and terms before or after registration. You cannot add them. If you file in Class 25 and later realise you needed Class 35 as well, the only route is an entirely new application, with a new fee and a new waiting period, while the gap sits open in the meantime.

That is why this page exists, and why we would rather you spent ten minutes here than ten minutes guessing.

Every term we suggest has already been accepted by the UK IPO

If you ask a general AI tool to write your goods and services, it will produce something plausible. That is what those tools do. The problem is that plausible is not the same as acceptable, and an examiner will tell you the difference several weeks later, after you have paid.

Our tools cannot do that, and not because we have asked them nicely.

They do not write terms at all. They select from a list of terms taken from real UK trademark registrations, every mark registered at the UK IPO in the last five years, and growing. The tool can only hand you something that a real business has already filed and the IPO has already accepted.

One deliberate choice sits behind that. We count only marks that reached registration, because a refused application is not evidence of anything. A term that appears here survived examination on somebody else's application.

We look back five years rather than further because examination practice moves. A term accepted a decade ago is weak evidence about what will be accepted now, and the set is rebuilt quarterly so it keeps pace.

What these tools are, and what they are not

These are guidance tools and they are free. They will get you a long way, and for a straightforward business they may well get you all the way.

What they cannot do is know your plans.

They cannot see that you are launching a second product line next spring, that you licence your name to a distributor in Ireland, or that the thing you most need to protect is a service you have not started selling yet. Those decisions change the answer, and they come from a conversation rather than a dataset.

So the tools do the heavy lifting and narrow the field. Before you commit money to an application, we would always recommend a trademark consultation and audit, where somebody looks at your business and signs off the classes and terms with you.

Classes and terms in other countries

Two routes exist for filing abroad, and classes and terms do not travel cleanly down either of them.

Route one

The Madrid Protocol

Through WIPO, one international application that designates the countries you want. We have written about the trade-off in more detail in our piece on the Madrid Protocol.

Route two

Directly to each local office

Applying country by country, through a trademark attorney or solicitor in each one. More moving parts, and more local knowledge behind each filing.

What both routes share is that every office has its own quirks about what wording it will accept, how specific it expects you to be, and what it charges. A specification that sails through in the UK can attract an objection elsewhere for reasons that have nothing to do with your business.

Our view is that there is no substitute for local expertise.

A local representative avoids the small mistakes that cause objections, and if your application is opposed they are already appointed, already familiar with the file, and able to handle it. That generally means a higher success rate, less delay, and less money spent fixing things.
